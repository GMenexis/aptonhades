# AptonHades
Two-Stroke quad from Hades. 

From a 3hp commuter to a 16hp monster.

This project is something very close to my heart. I am sharing this story as it constitutes the beginning of a great chapter in my life.

# Description of the project

<img src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/87b7487b-72d7-4f77-9c50-d818d6bdd59c" width="100%" alt="Περιγραφή του project μου">

The machine is an SMC Mustang 50 quad, a very basic commuter for everyday, simple use. As with every great project, people tend to take a chassis not intended to do what people want it to do. And that's fascinating! And that was my plan: to make this 50cc commuter a missile. But that wasn't the plan from the start.

Originally, I did not know anything about engines, about the principles governing their operation. Well, I knew the basics, but nothing more that would make me believe that I would be capable of interfering with a two-stroke engine. However, the sad day came, and the engine bailed on me while riding the quad. One of the saddest days of my life became a significant landmark in my development. The seized 50cc engine allowed me to dismantle it step by step and encounter the inner components of the engine. That was the beginning of an inner fire that started inside of me and still goes strong. To this very day!

All of the above are not very significant as far as explaining the project's development, but their significance to me is of great meaning, because it was the start of everything important I could actually enjoy and be somewhat proud of. Every first project is a major checkpoint in a builder's and engineer's life.


Enough with the above, it's time to talk with a sense of technicality. As mentioned before, we have an SMC Mustang 50, equipped with a Chinese Minarelli Horizontal engine, powered by the two-stroke cycle. This type of engine can be found in a large number of scooters, like the Yamaha Jog 50, the Yamaha Aerox, etc. In the beginning, I did not know the type of engine of the quad, which made it extremely difficult and stressful to find parts to fix the seized engine. The verification of the engine's identity was a crossover of me measuring parts from my engine and finding parts from the internet that matched exactly the silhouette of the cases and existing components that I had.


<img src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/6509727d-d21c-4b4a-92c7-8ce33de1aef7" width="100%" alt="Περιγραφή του project μου">

After knowing the engine's identity, I bought an Airsal Sport 70cc Cylinder kit, and after installation, I got my first victory, as the quad fired up after a significant amount of time. Due to the increase in displacement from stock 50cc to 70cc, the engine was much stronger from low to mid RPM, but at high RPM, the engine was choking. The reason for this behaviour was the stock old muffler designed by the factory to deliver low-end torque.

It is very important to mention that two-strokes rely heavily on the exhaust pipe to make power and burn the air-fuel mixture sufficiently. Because two-strokers operate with ports carved inside the cylinder walls, there is this thing called overlapping. That means that while the exhaust port opens, the intake port opens too, so the scavenging process and the filling of the cylinder with fresh air-fuel mixture happen simultaneously, thus providing a way for unburned mixture to escape from the exhaust port. This is very crucial in two-strokes, because efficiency drops heavily and power delivery is poor. 

That's why I went and bought one of the best pipes out there, the Yasuni Carrera 16 pipe, or C16. What an incredible exhaust pipe. When I first unboxed it, it gave me shivers just from looking at it. Anyway, originally, I could not install the pipe because there was not enough clearance between the engine and the chassis, so I made some space by cutting a big chunk of the chassis. I'm so disappointed by my actions back then, but mistakes happen, especially when someone is stressed and fed up with a part that will not fit. The fitment issues of the exhaust were known to me from before, because I bought a scooter exhaust to fit on an ATV. I know I'm problematic.

Anyway, I was able to fit the exhaust with the extra room created by cutting the chassis, and I enjoyed the quad for the summer. Although the setup was working, the tune was not spot on, and the parts could not support each other. The 70cc Airsal could never match the powerband of the high-revving pipe, resulting in a vehicle that was not that fast and not that enjoyable. In other words, it was still a commuter. It is very important to mention that it is possible for one to spend a lot of money and enjoy little to no significant power gains compared to the investment. Two-strokes, as someone told me, are like toxic girlfriends, in the sense that they need constant care and attention to detail. 

So after the summer, I took the big decision to dismantle the quad completely and chase perfection. I took off the engine, the chassis was stripped bare metal, every bolt removed, every plastic panel removed, all the suspension setup removed, everything. 

Let's start with the big boy, the engine. I dismantled all the engine parts until I was left with just the cases. I cleaned them thoroughly with hot water, engine degreaser, and lots of scrubbing. Afterward, I machined the cases and opened the crank area to match the new cylinder, the Stage6 Sport Pro MK2 70cc. With the machining complete, new C4 steel crank bearings were installed, along with the new circular Stage6 HPC crank for high primary compression, better and faster cylinder filling, and increased strength and stability at 16,000 RPM. 

The completion of the bottom end of the engine meant that it was time to fit the cylinder kit and measure the squish, a very important parameter for two-strokers. Squish is the distance between the piston crown at top dead center (TDC) and the squishband of the cylinder head. It is very critical to tune it properly and to specification so the engine can operate properly and deliver, reliably, the highest horsepower output. I measured my squish around 0.6mm, using solder wire within Stage6's specification (0.5 mm- 0.8 mm). To complete the top end, Vforce3 carbon reeds, a Motoforce inclined intake, and a 24mm carburetor were installed. The size of the carburetor is on the big side for the displacement, but the setup will be able to run at maximum physical revs, producing maximum output at this displacement and setup. 


With the completion of the top end, next is the CVT and the gearbox. For the CVT setup, I installed a Stage6 R/T oversize variator, with 5 gramm rollers. For the driven pulley, everything is stock except the contra spring, which is a very stiff red spring with a rating of 2000rpm. This particular combination of springs and rollers was essential to make the engine change gears at high RPM, in order to take full advantage of the Carrera 16 pipe with the irrationally high powerband. The secret in CVT setups is to tune it so the gear changes take place in the powerband rev range. In addition, I installed a reinforced Kevlar belt to match my power estimates properly and be able to transfer the power safely to the chunky rear tires. As far as the gearbox is concerned, I measured the output speed of the stock gearbox, given no-slip conditions at the variator and pulley phases, a 10,000 RPM operating engine speed, and the CVT fully maxed out. By fully maxed out, the Kevlar belt is at the top diameter of the variator and the smallest possible diameter at the back of the pulley. I was able to measure the top speed at around 50 km/h, so I decided to change the  secondary transmission gears to increase the theoretical top speed to 80 km/h. The reason for the gearbox modification is not to restrict the stronger and torquier engine. The increased power output will be able to pull longer gears effortlessly, thus ensuring lower RPM at higher speeds. To end the CVT-gearbox paragraph, the oversized variator could not clear the CVT cover, so machinery took place to make it fit. I exploited this opportunity to make venting channels to the CVT area to keep temperatures at a steady level. Every bearing in the motor was changed, and I mention this because it's very important for every builder to take it into serious consideration. So the engine is complete, and now the rest of the build can take place. Next in line was the chassis. For it, I had in mind to restore the damaged section for exhaust clearance and sandblast it to remove rust from the rusty, 20-year-old chassis. And that's how it went. It took an awful amount of time, but in the end, I ended up with a very clean, restored, and beautiful-looking metallic cage. And to top everything off, I took the cage for powder coating and ended up with a very beautiful, dark, vibrant blue finish, which you can see in the photos below.

At this point, I want to mention that the endeavour of restoring an old machine does not concentrate solely on the engine revival. A machine is not just an engine, it's a plethora of things that together get to be a working vehicle. To conclude my point, the significance of the engine department does not exceed, by any means, the significance of the chassis, and the importance of the suspension geometry and setup. You get my point.

Next in line was the final piece of the puzzle before the rebuild could take place. I am referring to the suspension of the four-wheeler, and it is a very complicated issue, as it will be discussed further down in the paragraph. The suspension of the SMC Mustang 50 consists of a single A-arm suspension in the front and a single fork setup at the back, as all of the ATVs. First and foremost, the suspension forks were restored to their former glory, as rust and gunk were devouring them. I repainted them graphite black, and the result was an interesting contrast between the chassis's vibrant blue and the suspension's black colour. In addition, I took some basic care of the coilovers so they look clean and readjusted their damping height to alter the stance of the vehicle. This choice was a very careful move, due to the nature of the tires the quad is running, as far as size is concerned, and the problems that come with a single A-arm suspension.

Single A-arm suspension suffers from a phenomenon called dynamic camber. This means that during turning, braking, and in general, at any given weight transfer process, the tires' angles change dynamically, thus having a great impact on grip and on the rolling effect of the chassis during turning. The ways to address the problems will be discussed in a new blog, as they have not been used on the vehicle's suspension setup. To give the reader a glimpse of some ideas I think will help significantly, I suggest installing an anti-roll bar and wheel spacers, lowering the vehicle's ride height, and selecting a more square tire type for the front. The reasons will be discussed at another time for the specific modifications.   

To give some sense of where the project is, the engine has been finished, the chassis has been restored, and the suspension parts are in great condition. So, logically, the next step is to start assembling the quad. And that's what I did. After one whole year of planning, machining, and stressing over every little detail that can go wrong in the engine's operation, I started the assembly process. It took me approximately one week of intense labour and measuring to make it one piece. I custom-fabricated an exhaust support bracket by welding because the bracket the exhaust came with could not clear the extra gearbox that ATVs have for the selection of Drive, Neutral, and Reverse. In addition, I exploited the magic of 3D-printing to create the little things missing from the build, like some brackets and restrictors. 

Last but definitely not least, the brakes were a headache. I could not find the drum cables that would fit my setup, so I had to improvise. I bought the cables from a Yamaha motorcycle that would match the stock cables in length, and I modified the drum receptors to be able to work with the altered cables

And now the time has come to analyze the behaviour and dynamics of the first ride, as well as the first impressions, due to the fact that I had not ridden the machine for over a year. I first interacted with the vehicle as a 50cc commuter, then I rode it for a while as a 70cc, improperly tuned two-stroke, and now the time had come to experience the beast. When I first took it for a test ride, everything clicked together. The motor was revving incredibly fast and in a snappy manner. I still cannot get used to the fact that with something less than a quarter throttle, the engine was already at 9000 RPM. The C16 was screaming at high revs, while being incredibly loud and with a metallic, high-pitched sound. It sounded like a proper two-stroke dirt bike. Last, but definitely not least, the acceleration of the quad was brutal. When I say brutal, I mean brutally brutal. The vehicle could not find traction on any type of road, except good asphalt. The rush when hitting the powerband, with the CVT working harmoniously with the pipe and the cylinder, is something that cannot be described. When hitting the powerband, I had to hold on to the handlebars to not get knocked over by the acceleration of the quad. It is important to mention that the 0-60 km/h time was under 4.6 seconds, so someone with some experience riding can truly reflect on the above numerical statement. And again, I want to mention that it is a setup never intended for this chassis, and that's why intense labour and all-around care were needed.

Although everything worked like a well-maintained gear clock, the setup of the suspension and the wheelbase were the limiting factors in going fast safely. That's why I would never push more than 60 km/h, because it felt extremely illegal and dangerous. The project is still ongoing because I desire to fix and upgrade the suspension setup and make the vehicle turn as if it's on rails. As I mentioned before, the next mods for Apton Hades are designated for the suspension, such as a custom anti-roll bar, lowering the ride height, changing to more square front tires, installing wheel spacers, and playing with preloaded camber and toe. Those modifications will be discussed at another time, in order to be addressed properly.

As with everything in life, it takes time, devotion, and effort to achieve the things someone desires. But always keep in mind and at heart, that nothing is impossible if someone truly wants it. I did not know a lot of things about engines and chassis dynamics, but this whole trip gave me a plethora of valuable lessons to develop my skills as an engineer and as a builder. I still cannot say that I know a lot of things, but there is one thing I can surely say. Man, I love what I'm doing, and I hope that anyone who is reading this is blessed in life by being able to do the things one loves.

Thank you
